Part 1: Estimating Cross-Sectional Regression Models

The Estimation Problem

To use \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\Sigma} in practice, we need to estimate them from data.

The simplest approach: use the sample mean and sample covariance of historical returns. We’ll discover what the drawbacks are from this approach in our case study.

Another approach (maybe the most common): cross-sectional regression models like CAPM.

The Estimation Problem

CAPM stands for the “Capital Asset Pricing Model”

CAPM says that each asset’s return is a linear function of one or more common factors.

This gives us more “efficient” estimates, especially when k is large relative to the sample size n. We’ll talk more about this in our case study.

The Estimation Problem

From a data scientist’s perspective, it’s just simple linear regression and parameter reduction.

From a finance person’s perspective, it’s basically gospel. They really use these “factor models” for a lot of the statistical modeling. It’s very common to run cross sectional regressions and call them “factor models.”

For the financial justification—equilibrium arguments, the market portfolio, and the derivation of the CAPM pricing equation—see Campbell (2018), ch. 3.

CAPM: The Simple Version

The Capital Asset Pricing Model says:

y_{t,i} = \alpha_i + \beta_i \cdot r_{t,\text{SPY}} + \epsilon_{t,i}

  • r_{t,\text{SPY}}: the “market return” (e.g. for S&P 500) at time t
  • \beta_i: asset i’s sensitivity to the market (“beta”)
  • \alpha_i: excess return not explained by the market (“alpha”)
  • \epsilon_{t,i}: idiosyncratic noise, \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t,i}] = 0
  • This is not a forecasting model! It’s not trying to forecast the future with historical data.

A high-beta fund/stock/investment amplifies market moves. A low-beta stock is more stable.

CAPM: Fitting One Asset

Should you YOLO tech stocks, or stick to more conservative investments?

From One Asset to Many: One Row

For a single time period t, the CAPM equation for all m assets at once is just m separate regressions sharing the same \mathbf{x}_t:

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_{t,1} & y_{t,2} & \cdots & y_{t,m} \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{row } t \text{ of } \mathbf{Y}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & r_{t,\text{SPY}} \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{row } t \text{ of } \mathbf{X}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \alpha_2 & \cdots & \alpha_m \\ \beta_1 & \beta_2 & \cdots & \beta_m \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B}} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \epsilon_{t,1} & \epsilon_{t,2} & \cdots & \epsilon_{t,m} \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{row } t \text{ of } \mathbf{E}}

Each column of \mathbf{B} is one asset’s (\alpha_i, \beta_i)^\intercal. Stack n such rows and you get the full matrix equation.

From One Asset to a Portfolio: Multivariate Regression

When we have m assets to model simultaneously, we stack them into a matrix:

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_{1,1} & \cdots & y_{1,m} \\ y_{2,1} & \cdots & y_{2,m} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ y_{n,1} & \cdots & y_{n,m} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{Y},\; n \times m} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & r_{1,\text{SPY}} \\ 1 & r_{2,\text{SPY}} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & r_{n,\text{SPY}} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{X},\; n \times (r+1)} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \cdots & \alpha_m \\ \beta_1 & \cdots & \beta_m \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{B},\; (r+1) \times m} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \epsilon_{1,1} & \cdots & \epsilon_{1,m} \\ \epsilon_{2,1} & \cdots & \epsilon_{2,m} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \epsilon_{n,1} & \cdots & \epsilon_{n,m} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{E},\; n \times m}

\underbrace{\mathbf{Y}}_{n \times m} = \underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{n \times (r+1)} \underbrace{\mathbf{B}}_{(r+1) \times m} + \underbrace{\mathbf{E}}_{n \times m}

where row t of \mathbf{Y} is \mathbf{y}_t^\intercal, row t of \mathbf{X} is \mathbf{x}_t^\intercal (the factor values at time t).

Regarding the errors, rows are completely independent from one another, but within a row, \text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_y.

Estimating B and \Sigma_y

Because the same predictors appear in every column of \mathbf{Y}, we can estimate \mathbf{B} column by column using OLS:

\hat{\mathbf{B}} = (\mathbf{X}^\intercal \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\intercal \mathbf{Y}

The residual covariance is then:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_y = \frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{B}})^\intercal (\mathbf{Y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\mathbf{B}}) 

# Multivariate OLS: same X for all m dependent variables
B_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, Y, rcond=None)[0]   # (r+1, m)
resids = Y - X @ B_hat                           # (n, m)
Sigma_hat = resids.T @ resids / len(resids)      # (m, m)

Part 2: From Regression Output to Forecast Moments

From Regression to Forecast Moments

Given estimates \hat{\mathbf{B}} and \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_y, what are \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\Sigma}?

The law of total expectation says that if our regression model is true, then

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1} \mid \text{past}] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_{t+1}]\mid \text{past}\right] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{B}^\intercal \mathbf{x}_{t+1}\mid \text{past}] = \mathbf{B}^\intercal \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\mid \text{past}]

Plug in whatever estimate for \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\mid \text{past}] you want, but the simplest is the historical average:

\boldsymbol{\mu} \approx \hat{\mathbf{B}}^\intercal \bar{\mathbf{x}}

Our next unit on time series models will be helpful for this, too!

From Regression to Forecast Moments

The law of total (co)variance says

\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1} \mid \text{past}] = \mathbb{V}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t+1}]\mid \text{past}] + \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t+1}]\mid \text{past}] ,

With our model estimates:

\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1} \mid \text{past}] \approx \hat{\mathbf{B}}^\intercal \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_X \hat{\mathbf{B}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_y

  • \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_X is some estimate of future factor covariance (e.g. historical covariance matrix)
  • \hat{\mathbf{B}}^\intercal \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_X \hat{\mathbf{B}}: variance coming from uncertainty in the factor (shared across all assets)
  • \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_y: idiosyncratic variance, the part no factor explains

Full CAPM Example in Python

import pandas as pd, numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

sectors = ["XLC","XLY","XLP","XLE","XLF","XLV","XLI","XLB","XLRE","XLK","XLU"]

data = pd.read_csv("../data/prices.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True)
rets = data[sectors + ["SPY"]].pct_change().dropna() * 100

Y = rets[sectors].values           # (n, 11)
X = sm.add_constant(rets["SPY"].values)  # (n, 2)

B_hat   = np.linalg.lstsq(X, Y, rcond=None)[0]   # (2, 11)
resids  = Y - X @ B_hat
Sigma_y = resids.T @ resids / len(resids)

x_bar = X.mean(axis=0)            # (2,) — average [1, SPY_return]
m_vec = B_hat.T @ x_bar           # (11,) forecast mean vector

Sigma_X = np.cov(X.T)             # (2, 2)
S_mat   = B_hat.T @ Sigma_X @ B_hat + Sigma_y   # (11, 11) forecast cov

Special Case: Intercept Only

If we use \mathbf{x}_t^\intercal = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \end{bmatrix} (no factors, just an intercept), then:

\hat{\mathbf{B}} = \bar{\mathbf{Y}}^\intercal \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boldsymbol{\mu} \approx \bar{Y}

and

\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{y}_{t+1} \mid \text{past}] \approx \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_y = \frac{1}{n}(\mathbf{Y} - \bar{\mathbf{Y}})^\intercal(\mathbf{Y} - \bar{\mathbf{Y}})

This is exactly the sample mean and sample covariance. The CAPM regression is a structured way to get better estimates when factor structure is present.

Part 3: From Forecast Moments to Portfolio Weights

The Optimization Problem

We now have estimates of \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\Sigma}. We also have control over over \mathbf{w}. How should we choose it?

We’ll we have to balance risk and reward!

  • reward: \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\mu}
  • risk: \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}

The Optimization Problem

  • reward: \mathbf{w}^\intercal\boldsymbol{\mu}
  • risk: \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}

We need to “combine” them into one “goodness/satisfaction” number. Twiddle \mathbf{w} to maximize U(\mathbf{w}).

Unfortunately, different investors have different risk tolerances, so there is no single “right” answer.

The Optimization Problem

We parameterize preferences with risk aversion \gamma > 0 and maximize:

U(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \, \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}

There’s also a constraint.

Maximize this subject to \mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} = 1.

Sometimes the constraint is written g(\mathbf{w}) = 0.

In this case, g(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} - 1

The Optimization Problem

U(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \, \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}

  • Large \gamma: investor strongly penalizes variance → conservative portfolio
  • Small \gamma: investor chases return → aggressive portfolio
  • Don’t memorize rules of thumb. Try a few. Data scale dependent.

This is the mean-variance utility from Markowitz (1952). For a broader discussion of utility functions, risk aversion coefficients, and the expected utility framework that motivates this form, see Campbell (2018), ch. 1. For worked examples in small-dimensional settings (one risky + one riskless asset, two risky assets), see Campbell (2018), ch. 2.

The Risk-Return Tradeoff

Before solving, let’s visualize: for random weights, what (risk, return) pairs are achievable?

There is a clear frontier — the efficient frontier — that we can trace out analytically.

Lagrange Multipliers: The Objective

For two assets we can draw U(w_1, w_2) as contours. The unconstrained maximum is the center of the ellipses.

Lagrange Multipliers: Adding the Constraint

The constraint w_1 + w_2 = 1 is a line. The constrained optimum is where the line is tangent to a contour — meaning \nabla U and \nabla g point in the same direction

Lagrange Multipliers

At the optimum, \nabla U = \lambda \nabla g, i.e.

\nabla U(\mathbf{w}) - \lambda \, \nabla g(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{0}

But this is exactly the critical condition for the unconstrained problem of maximizing

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = U(\mathbf{w}) - \lambda \, g(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \, \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w} - \lambda(\mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} - 1)

since \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \nabla U - \lambda \nabla g = \mathbf{0} and \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = -(g(\mathbf{w}) - 0) = 0 enforces the constraint.

we call \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) the Lagrangian

Solving with Lagrange Multipliers

To maximize U(\mathbf{w}) subject to \mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} = 1, form the Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \underbrace{\mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{1}{2}\gamma \, \mathbf{w}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w}}_{U(\mathbf{w})} - \lambda \underbrace{(\mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} - 1)}_{g(\mathbf{w})}

Set partial derivatives to zero:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \boldsymbol{\mu} - \gamma \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w} - \lambda \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0} \tag{1}

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda} = 1 - \mathbf{1}^\intercal \mathbf{w} = 0 \tag{2}

Solving for \mathbf{w}^*

From equation (1):

\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu} - \lambda \mathbf{1}) \tag{3}

Substitute into equation (2) and solve for \lambda:

\lambda = \frac{\mathbf{1}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu} - \gamma}{\mathbf{1}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \mathbf{1}} \tag{4}

Substitute (4) back into (3):

\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{1}{\gamma} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} \left[ \boldsymbol{\mu} - \frac{\mathbf{1}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu} - \gamma}{\mathbf{1}^\intercal \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \mathbf{1} \right]

Optimal Weights in Python

def mpt_weights(mu, Sigma, gamma, s=1.0):
    Sinv  = np.linalg.inv(Sigma)
    ones  = np.ones(len(mu))
    q1    = ones @ Sinv @ mu
    q2    = ones @ Sinv @ ones
    lam   = (q1 - gamma * s) / q2
    return Sinv @ (mu - lam * ones) / gamma


w_star = mpt_weights(m_vec, S_mat, gamma=1.0)
print("Optimal weights:", w_star.round(4))
print("Expected return:", w_star @ m_vec)
print("Std. deviation :", np.sqrt(w_star @ S_mat @ w_star))

The Efficient Frontier

As we vary \gamma from \infty (minimum variance) down toward 0 (maximum return), we trace the efficient frontier — the set of portfolios that are optimal for some risk aversion.

gammas = np.logspace(-6, 6, 2000)

opt_means = np.array([mpt_weights(m_vec, S_mat, g) @ m_vec for g in gammas])
opt_sds   = np.array([np.sqrt(mpt_weights(m_vec, S_mat, g) @ S_mat @ mpt_weights(m_vec, S_mat, g)) for g in gammas])

# clip frontier to the scatter's range so neither drowns the other
sd_max  = np.percentile(all_sds,   99) * 1.2
mu_max  = np.percentile(all_means, 99) * 1.3
mu_min  = np.percentile(all_means,  1) - abs(np.percentile(all_means, 1)) * 0.2
mask    = (opt_sds <= sd_max) & (opt_means <= mu_max) & (opt_means >= mu_min)

plt.scatter(all_sds, all_means, alpha=0.2, s=5, label="Random portfolios")
plt.plot(opt_sds[mask], opt_means[mask], color="red", linewidth=2, label="Efficient frontier")
plt.xlim(0, sd_max)
plt.ylim(mu_min, mu_max)
plt.xlabel("Std. deviation (risk)")

plt.legend()

Key Takeaways

  1. A portfolio’s return is \mathbf{w}^\intercal \mathbf{y}_{t+1}, a scalar formed by weighting k assets.

  2. The first two moments of the forecast distribution — \boldsymbol{\mu} (mean vector) and \boldsymbol{\Sigma} (covariance matrix) — are sufficient for mean-variance analysis.

  3. Cross-sectional regression (CAPM) gives a structured way to estimate \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\Sigma} by decomposing returns into a factor component and idiosyncratic noise.

  4. MPT turns the estimation problem into an optimization: given \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\Sigma}, the closed-form optimal weights \mathbf{w}^* maximize mean-variance utility for a given risk aversion \gamma.

  5. Sweeping over \gamma traces the efficient frontier — the set of portfolios that minimize variance for a given expected return.
